# Background: the thinking behind the Hopeboard

This file records why the Hopeboard exists and the ideas it teaches, so the build keeps the point and not only the layout.

## Who it's for

The board is for ordinary people who read a stock chart as "what is happening now": a line that goes up over time. Its point is that a price is a bet on the next ten years, and to make that bet readable without finance training. It builds the reader's judgement; it doesn't hand out picks. The board does not "sell the line": the price chart and the stock's ten-year return are never shown as headline numbers.

## The two numbers behind every chart

A stock's price chart is two numbers multiplied together:

- **The record:** what the business actually earned, per share, and how fast that grew. Checkable, slow, indifferent to opinion.
- **The hope:** how many years of that profit people will pay for. Jumpy, and entirely opinion.

A chart shows only the product, so a rising line looks like one fact when it is two. Jack Bogle's split of stock returns into "investment return" (dividend yield plus earnings growth) and "speculative return" (the change in the P/E) is the same idea. The board doesn't use that vocabulary: no "mood", no "multiple", no "sentiment". Its own words are "priced at N years of last year's profit" and "the price needs profit to grow X%/year". "Hope" survives only in the name.

## Why required growth

If the price already assumes a lot, growth that arrives is not extra. It is what the buyer paid for. A stock returns roughly its profit growth plus the dividends it pays, adjusted for any change in how many years of profit people pay. So for any price you can work backwards to the growth it needs for a normal stock-market return. That is the board's central number. It answers "is this a reasonable price?" without forecasting anything.

The yardstick is the S&P 500's long-run average (10%/year since 1928), which itself sits above the safe US bond. The extra return over the bond is the payment for risk. The board lets a reader see, card by card, whether a price leaves any cushion above the bond.

## What hindsight shows

- **Cisco, March 2000.** Priced at 150 years of profit, needing 36%/year growth. It delivered 14%/year, which is good growth and not enough. The stock took about 25 years to get back to that price.
- **Apple, May 2016.** Priced at 11 years of profit, needing almost no growth. Sales had just fallen 8%, and the market priced it as a fading phone-maker. Profit then grew 15%/year per share. This is what "cheap" looked like at the time, and it felt like a dying company.

## Parking, not betting

The board is framed as where people park money for a decade, because that is how ordinary people think about the bond, gold and the index. It then shows what each place actually promises. Safety is a property of the price, not a feeling about the company: a famous company at a high price can pay less than the safe US bond. Gold sits among the baselines because people park money there, and it is the odd one out because it has no profit at all.

## Taste rules

Treat these as rules for any new copy or design.

- No AI-sounding lines: the "X, not Y" turn, aphorisms, taglines, filler that explains the concept, intro text on the page.
- No esoteric or unclear words: margin of safety, mood, "pts", "the ask", "room for error".
- No numbers without an anchor: scenario growth rates must come from something a reader can check.
- No design noise: the dollar share price, labels that need explaining, uneven cards.
