# Philosophy: how the owner thinks about markets and about teaching

This file records the reasoning behind the board in plain words, so the build carries the thinking and not only the layout. Figures quoted here are illustrations from the design stage and have not all been checked. Verify any of them before showing one in the app.

## The view in one line

A price is the market's bet on the next ten years. The useful question is what that bet requires to come true.

## What a price is

- A price re-sets every day with everything known. On any date it contains the market's guess for the next decade.
- A stock's move over a year has three parts: the normal payment for holding risk (roughly 8–10% a year for stocks, built in as a slope), the surprise against what the price assumed, and the change in how many years of profit people will pay.
- Growth that is expected is already paid for. A company growing a lot is not news. A company growing more than its price assumed is. Cisco grew its profit for fifteen years after 2000 and the stock went sideways, because the price had assumed more.
- A stock returns roughly its profit growth plus the cash it pays out, adjusted for any change in the years people pay. Apple's profit per share grew ~15% a year over the last decade (roughly 10% for the whole company before buybacks) while the stock did ~25–28% a year. The gap is the years people pay going from ~11 to ~38. That re-rating happened once and cannot repeat the same way. From here, a buyer gets roughly profit growth plus buybacks plus the small dividend, unless the years paid move again.
- Markets are good at facts and weaker on how long a good thing lasts. "This can't last" is a stubborn bias, and buying good businesses priced as if they won't last is the respectable version of picking stocks.

## What a chart hides

- A stock chart is two numbers multiplied together: what the business earned, and how many years of that profit people paid. It plots the product and throws the factors away.
- Two companies can have identical charts and be different bets. A smooth rising line looks like compounding and invites the reader to extrapolate, including the part that cannot repeat.
- Professionals learn this early (the CFA curriculum, Bogle's split of returns into investment return and speculative return, Mauboussin's expectations investing, and the first bear market, when a good company's stock halves while its profit rises). It does not reach ordinary readers because the people who sell charts earn from trading. "The stock did 12×" gets trades. "Seven points of that was hope, which may reverse" does not.
- The board therefore never shows the price line alone, and never as a headline.

## Picking winners is the wrong game

- The base rate: about nine in ten professional US stock funds trailed the S&P 500 over a recent decade (unverified; S&P's SPIVA scorecard is the source to check). The index is a way to own Apple without having been right about Apple in 2000.
- Apple since 2000 beat the index by a huge margin. It also fell by a third to four-fifths six times along the way (about −80% in the dot-com crash, −57% in 2008, and roughly −30% to −40% in 2012–13, 2018, 2020 and 2022). The return went to people who did not sell. The crisis years cannot be excluded, because the holder lived through them.
- The obvious names of the day usually disappoint. The companies at the top of the market in 2000 (Cisco, Intel, Microsoft, Lucent and others) and in 2016 (GE, IBM and others) mostly trailed. Over the long record, the largest companies at the start of a decade tend to trail the market by a percent or two a year over the next one. The feeling of obviousness attaches to whoever is on top, and being on top is already in the price.
- Reasons, not names. In 2016 Apple was priced at ~11 years of profit as a fading hit, after its first revenue fall in years. That was a reason: a franchise priced as if it were a dud. A good business priced as a mediocre one is the kind of pick with a decent base rate behind it. At ~35 years that argument is gone. The honest tell that a pick is the real thing is that buying it feels slightly embarrassing.
- The "Magnificent 7" beat the index over the past decade, but the label was coined in 2023, and the 2016 list was different. The price decided it: in 2016 these were priced as good companies and turned out to be monopolies. The top ten of 2000 were priced as monopolies and turned out to be good companies. Today the large names are priced as monopolies, so that re-rating is already banked.

## Hindsight makes it look easy

- "It's Apple!" is the 2026 status of the brand placed back in 2016. The 2016 version was "peak iPhone", with a big investor selling out.
- Buying is the easy part. Holding through the falls, and not selling a third of it after it doubles, is the hard part.
- The winner is remembered. The dozen stocks that felt equally obvious and went nowhere are forgotten.
- Inside knowledge of a product is a real edge, and the only kind a private investor has over the index.

## Reading what a price assumes

- For any stock, write one or two sentences on what the market has priced it for. Then there are only three honest positions. Accept that story and take the normal return. Think the story is wrong and be able to say why. Or have no opinion and buy the whole market, which is the average story at the average return.
- If the sentence cannot be written in profit terms, the price is carrying a story rather than a forecast. That is where bubbles live.
- A falling number of years paid is not a falling business. Oracle's business did not shrink in a year when its stock fell; the belief did. A rising stock is not proof that the business improved. It proves expectations rose.
- The same price gives two numbers depending on the profit used. Last year's profit is in the bank. Next year's forecast is in a spreadsheet. "It's cheap at 20" quotes use the forecast, which assumes the forecast arrives.
- Plain readings the owner liked: Apple at ~35 years means profit grows a little every year with no stumble; Cisco at 150 in 2000 meant 30–50% growth for fifteen years with no competition, and Cisco did grow nicely while the stock went nowhere for years; a utility at ~15 means 3% growth forever; the S&P at ~40 on the long-run measure means record margins hold and nothing goes wrong with the top ten.

## Risk is the price of leaving the bond or the index

- The safe US bond is the price of taking no risk. The index is the average of everyone's story, with the average return (~10% a year since 1928). Anything else has to be paid for taking extra risk, so it has to promise more.
- The first question about a company is therefore how much growth its price requires, and whether that is more or less than the business has shown.
- Safety is a property of the price, not a feeling about the company. A famous company at a high price can pay less than the safe US bond.
- People park money in gold, bonds and the index. Gold has no profit at all. Its price is a bet on other people's fear, and it went twenty years without gaining.
- "Parking money for a decade" is the frame: the board shows what each place people park money actually promises.

## How the owner wants an analyst to behave

- Statements, not questions. No disclaimers and no hedging about who is speaking.
- Simple language. The aim is understanding and dispelling myths, not technical review.
- Be precise. The detail is where mistakes hide, and generic points pattern-matched from other topics have been caught and conceded more than once.
- The owner pushes back on what they know. The weak spot is what they do not know, so flag soft claims.
- First-hand evidence outweighs the story.
- Push back on the owner when something is wrong, with the reason.
- Use one correct term for each thing so clarity never drops.

## Teaching principles for the app

- Build the reader's judgement. Do not hand out picks, and do not sell a line or a headline return.
- Plain words, concrete pictures, no jargon. If a pro term is needed, translate it once and keep to the board's own terms.
- Show the two numbers every time: what the business has done, and what the price assumes.
- Measure everything against the safe US bond and the index.
- Turn every number into a consequence: what you would make or lose.
- Anchor every scenario to something a reader can check.
- Flag soft claims on the card or in the footer. "Reasonably priced" is not advice, and the app should say so.
- Every word does a job, and nothing on the page is there to sound clever.
- Card wording follows `docs/writing-guide.md`.
